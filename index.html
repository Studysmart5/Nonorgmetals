Overview

Core Idea

Microbiology is the study of microscopic organisms. The lecture covers how microbes are studied (stains and microscopes), the history of the field, its pure and applied branches, and the core vocabulary of infection: types and stages of infection, pathogens, pathogenicity, virulence, toxins, and infection by normal bacterial flora.

Learning Points

  • Microscopic organisms are unicellular, a cell cluster, or acellular, and must be viewed with a microscope or electron microscope.
  • The microbiological procedure has two routes: stains (basic simple, basic compound) and microscopes (EM, light).
  • History has an Ancient period (before the microscope) and a Modern period (after its invention): Hooke, Leeuwenhoek, Pasteur, Cohn, Koch.
  • Branches are pure (taxonomic and integrative arrangements) and applied (practical benefits).
  • Infection is classified by order, duration, spread, and number of pathogens.
  • Stages of infection: incubation, prodromal, invasion, convalescent.
  • Key pathogen terms: pathogen, pathogenicity, true vs opportunistic, pathologic, virulence, virulence factor, toxigenicity.
  • Normal flora can act as potential pathogens; infection rarely leads to infectious disease, and host defenses are the most important factor in the outcome.

Key Definitions

  • Microbiology: from Greek mikros (“small”), bios (“life”), -logia (study). The study of microscopic organisms, defined as any living organism that is either a single cell (unicellular), a cell cluster, or has no cells at all (acellular), and must be viewed with the aid of a microscope or electron microscope.
  • Microbiologist: a specialist in microbiology.
  • Infection: the entry, establishment, and multiplication of pathogenic organisms within a host.
  • Infectious disease: the state of damage or toxicity in the body caused by an infectious agent.
  • Portal of entry: route of entry for an infectious agent; typically a cutaneous or membranous route.
  • Pathogen: any agent (usually a virus, bacterium, fungus, protozoan, or helminth) that causes a disease.
  • Pathogenicity: the capacity of microbes to cause a disease or infection.
  • Pathologic: capable of inducing physical damage to the host.
  • Virulence: in infection, the relative capacity of a pathogen to invade and harm host cells.
  • Virulence factor: a microbe’s structures or capabilities that allow it to establish itself in a host and cause damage.
  • Toxigenicity: the tendency of a pathogen to produce toxins.

Classification / Types

1. Microbiological procedure

  • Stains: Basic simple; Basic compound
  • Microscope: EM (electron microscope); Light

2. Historical view

  • Ancient (before the discovery of the microscope): the existence of microorganisms was hypothesized for many centuries.
  • Modern (after the microscope invention).

3. Branches of microbiology: pure and applied

Pure microbiology: taxonomic arrangement

  • Bacteriology: bacteria
  • Mycology: fungi
  • Protozoology: protozoa
  • Phycology (algology): algae
  • Parasitology: parasites
  • Immunology: the immune system
  • Virology: viruses
  • Nematology: nematodes

Pure microbiology: integrative arrangement

  • Microbial cytology: microscopic and submicroscopic details of microorganisms.
  • Microbial physiology: how the microbial cell functions biochemically; includes microbial growth, metabolism, and cell structure.
  • Microbial ecology: the relationship between microorganisms and their environment.
  • Microbial genetics: how genes are organized and regulated in microbes in relation to their cellular functions.

Applied microbiology

  • Practical benefits (see Examples).

4. Types of infection

  • Primary: an initial infection in a previously healthy individual, later complicated by an additional (secondary) infection.
  • Secondary: an infection that compounds a preexisting one.
  • Acute: rapid onset and short duration.
  • Chronic: any process or disease that persists over a long duration.
  • Localized: a microbe enters a specific tissue, infects it, and remains confined there.
  • Systemic: invades many compartments and organs via the circulation; occurs throughout the body.
  • Focal: an infectious agent breaks loose from a localized infection and is carried by the circulation to other tissues.
  • Mixed (also known as synergistic): several different pathogens interact simultaneously to produce an infection.

5. Types of pathogens

  • True (primary) pathogens: microbes capable of causing infection and disease in healthy persons with normal immune defenses.
  • Opportunistic pathogens: cause disease when the host’s defenses are compromised, or when they become established in a part of the body that is natural to them.

6. Toxin-related conditions

  • Toxinoses: disease whose adverse effects are primarily due to production and release of toxins.
  • Toximias (toxin + blood): a toxin (microbial or otherwise) is spread throughout the bloodstream.
  • Intoxications (botulism): caused by ingestion of toxins.

Important Concepts

  • Infection and its outcome: infection does not necessarily lead to infectious disease; in fact, it probably rarely does. The outcome depends on (1) route of entry, (2) number of infectious bacteria, and (3) status of the host defenses (most importantly).
  • Normal flora as potential pathogens: in humans, some normal bacterial flora (Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae) live in a commensal or parasitic relationship without producing disease. They do not cause disease unless an opportunity is brought on by some compromise or weakness in the host’s anatomical barriers, tissue resistance, or immunity.
  • Transmission: these bacteria can be transmitted from one host to another, giving them additional opportunities to colonize or infect.
  • Endogenous bacterial disease: when a member of the normal flora causes an infectious disease; a disease brought on by bacteria “from within”.
  • Where bacteria are found: the normal flora, as well as any “contaminating” bacteria from the environment, are found on the body surfaces; the blood and internal tissues are sterile.
  • When infection occurs: if a bacterium, whether or not a component of the normal flora, breaches one of these surfaces, an infection is said to have occurred.
  • Primary then secondary: a primary infection in a healthy person may later be complicated by a secondary infection that compounds it.

Processes / Stages

Stages of infection (in order)

  1. Incubation period: from the initial contact with an infectious agent to the appearance of the first symptoms.
  2. Prodromal stage: a short period of mild symptoms at the end of the period of incubation; indicates the onset of disease.
  3. Period of invasion: during a clinical infection, the agent multiplies at high levels, exhibits its greatest toxicity, and becomes well established in the target tissues.
  4. Convalescent period: recovery; the period between the end of the disease and the complete restoration of health in a patient.

Historical sequence

Ancient period (hypothesized existence) → Modern period (after the microscope): Hooke (1660s) → Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723) → Pasteur (1822–1895) → Cohn (1828–1898) → Koch (1843–1910).

Examples

Benefits of applied microbiology

  • Industrial fermentation: e.g. production of alcohol, vinegar (Acetobacteraceae), and dairy products.
  • Antibiotic production: the beta-lactam antibacterials, which include the penicillins (produced by fungi in the genus Penicillium) and the cephalosporins.
  • Vehicles for cloning in more complex organisms such as plants.
  • Biotechnological production of important enzymes such as Taq polymerase; reporter genes for use in other genetic systems; novel molecular biology techniques such as the yeast two-hybrid system.
  • Biosynthesis of xanthan, alginate, and cellulose.
  • Microbial biodegradation or bioremediation of domestic, agricultural, and industrial wastes and subsurface pollution in soils, sediments, and marine environments.
  • Probiotics (bacteria potentially beneficial to the digestive system) and/or prebiotics: various claims concern their contributions to human and animal health.

Other examples

  • Normal flora that are potential pathogens: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae.
  • Intoxication: botulism.
  • Classic opportunistic infections in humans: dental caries and periodontal disease caused by normal flora of the oral cavity.

Clinical / Practical Importance

  • Members of the normal flora can cause endogenous bacterial disease when the host’s anatomical barriers, tissue resistance, or immunity are compromised.
  • Dental caries and periodontal disease are classic opportunistic infections caused by normal flora of the oral cavity.
  • Normal flora bacteria can be transmitted from one host to another, giving additional opportunities to colonize or infect.
  • Toxin-related conditions differ in mechanism: toxinoses (toxin production and release), toximias (toxin in the bloodstream), intoxications such as botulism (ingested toxin).
  • Outcome of infection depends on route of entry, number of infectious bacteria, and, most importantly, the status of the host defenses.
  • Applied microbiology provides antibiotics (penicillins, cephalosporins), enzymes (Taq polymerase), fermentation products, bioremediation, and probiotics/prebiotics.
  • Pasteurization prevents spoilage of food by bacteria.
