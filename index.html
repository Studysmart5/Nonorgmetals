Core Idea

Inorganic Pharmaceutical Chemistry studies the elements and their compounds (except carbon) that are useful in prevention, therapy, or medicament preparation. The lecture builds the atomic and periodic-table basis (atomic structure, electronic configuration, ionization, periodic law), then focuses on the alkali metals (Li, Na, K): their chemistry, and the medicinal use of lithium (bipolar disorder), sodium (body fluids, dehydration, pH) and potassium (hypokalemia, acidosis, hyperkalaemia).

Learning Points

  • Inorganic pharmaceuticals are used as therapeutic agents, pharmaceutical aids, pH modifiers of body fluid, replenishers of body fluids, reagents, and analytical titrants.
  • Protons define the element, electrons define the charge, neutrons define the isotope; all chemistry happens at the electronic level.
  • Alkali metals are group 1 (except hydrogen), s-block, one outer s electron, forming M⁺.
  • Alkali metals are not used directly (violent reaction in aqueous media); their salts are the medicinal forms.
  • Li salts: effective in mania/BD but with a very narrow therapeutic window and important drug interactions.
  • Na⁺: main extracellular cation; controls fluid volume, nerve impulses, muscle contraction, osmotic gradients.
  • K⁺: mainly intracellular (95%); narrow plasma window (3.5–5.0 mmol); both hypo- and hyperkalaemia are serious.

Key Definitions

  • Inorganic chemistry: the study of all the elements and their compounds except carbon and its compounds (studied under organic chemistry).
  • Pharmaceutical: any chemical substance useful in preventive or therapeutic use, or used in the preparation of medicament. Substances used only in the laboratory during preparation, and not present in the final product, are also included.
  • Subatomic particles: particles that make up an atom (sub- = “smaller size”). The atom = central nucleus surrounded by orbitals occupying discrete regions of space.
  • Nucleons: protons and neutrons collectively. Virtually all atomic mass is in the nucleus because electrons weigh so little.
  • Atomic number = No. of protons. Mass number (= nucleon number) = No. of protons + No. of neutrons.
  • Electronic configuration: the arrangement of electrons in an atom; electrons fill the atomic orbitals.
  • Valence electrons: electrons in the outer/last shell or energy level (beyond the stable core).
  • Anion: atom that gains electron(s) → negative (electrons > protons); nonmetals can gain electrons. Cation: metal atom that loses electron(s) → positive.
  • Periodic Law: when elements are arranged by increasing atomic number, there is periodic repetition of their physical and chemical properties.
  • Alkali metals: members of group 1 (first vertical column) of the periodic table, except hydrogen.
  • Renal tubular acidosis: the body accumulates acid because the kidneys fail to regulate the pH of urine and blood plasma.
  • Hyponatremia / Hypernatremia: low / high plasma sodium.
  • Hypokalemia: low K⁺ in blood plasma. Hyperkalaemia: increased K⁺ in plasma.
  • Hyperchloremia: increased chloride plasma levels. Acidosis: low pH of the blood plasma.
  • Polyuria: excess production of urine. Diabetes insipidus: a lithium adverse effect (see Clinical).

Classification / Types

1. Importance of inorganic pharmaceuticals (with examples)

  • Therapeutic purpose: astringents, antimicrobials.
  • Pharmaceutical aids: bentonite, talc.
  • Change reaction of body fluid (acidify or alkalinize): antacids, alkalis, mineral acids.
  • Replace/replenish normal body-fluid content: sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, phosphate.
  • Reagents: catalysts (platinum, nickel); oxidizing and reducing agents (lithium aluminum hydride).
  • Pharmaceutical analysis: titrants such as potassium permanganate.

2. Parts of the atom

  • Nucleus (most of the mass): protons (positive), neutrons (uncharged).
  • Orbitals: electrons in the electron cloud (negative).

3. Periodic table blocks and categories

  • Blocks: s-block, p-block, d-block, f-block.
  • Max electrons per subshell: s = 2, p = 6, d = 10, f = 14.
  • Categories shown in the table: alkali metals, alkaline-earth metals, transition metals, other metals, other nonmetals, halogens, noble gases, rare-earth elements, lanthanoid and actinoid elements.

4. Alkali metals (group 1)

  • Li (3), Na (11), K (19), Rb (37), Cs (55), Fr (87). Lecture focus: Li, Na, K.

5. Alkali metal compounds of medicinal interest

  • Halides, some oxides, carbonates, citrates and other salts.
  • Li: lithium carbonate Li₂CO₃, lithium citrate.
  • Na: sodium chloride, sodium bicarbonate, sodium citrate.
  • K: KCl, potassium bicarbonate, potassium citrate, tablets/capsules.

6. Hyperkalaemia treatment options

  • Calcium gluconate (IV): minimizes effects of hyperkalaemia on the heart.
  • Soluble insulin (IV): promotes shift of K⁺ into cells.
  • Diuretics: increase K⁺ secretion in the kidneys.
  • Dialysis: good option if urgent treatment is required.
  • Ion-exchange resins (polystyrene sulfonate resins): mild to moderate hyperkalaemia, only if no ECG changes.

Important Concepts

Atom, configuration, ionization

  • Neutral atom: No. of electrons = No. of protons (O: 8 p, 8 e; Cl: 17 p, 17 e). Notation: mass number top-left, atomic number bottom-left (199F).
  • Aufbau (diagonal rule) filling order: 1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d < 6p < 7s < 5f < 6d < 7p.
  • Shorthand configuration references the last completed shell (all orbitals with the same principal quantum number n filled).
  • Ion size: cations are always smaller than the original atom; anions are always larger.

Chemical behavior of alkali metals

  1. Silvery white, except caesium (golden yellow).
  2. Soft metals (can be cut with a knife); softness increases within the group.
  3. Very reactive, oxidize in air; reactivity increases within the group.
  4. React violently with water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen: 2Li(s) + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂(g)↑.
  5. Pharmaceutical use: not directly usable because of their reaction behavior in aqueous media; salts are used (NaCl and KCl solutions as oral rehydration salts; KCl also for potassium depletion). Na and K salts treat dehydration and restore mineral balance; Li salts treat BD.

Sodium: main biological roles

  1. Maintenance of body fluids: regulates body fluid via osmosis; >90% of all ions in plasma and interstitial fluid; the most abundant cation in extracellular fluid (Na⁺ controls extracellular volume). The kidneys regulate fluid level and the filtration, secretion and re-absorption of Na⁺ in the nephrons (functional unit of the kidney).
  2. Neurons and nerve impulses: Na⁺ builds an electrostatic potential across membranes with K⁺ as counter-ion; this potential allows transmission of nerve impulses.
  3. Muscle contraction and enzyme action: Na⁺ establishes osmotic gradients (water balance). Kidneys sense decreases in blood pressure and Na⁺; hormones (renin, antidiuretic hormones (ADH), atrial natriuretic peptide) are released to control blood pressure, osmotic balance and water retention.

Sodium: drugs, diet, toxicity

  • NaCl solutions for sodium depletion and dehydration: mostly IV; in chronic mild to moderate loss, NaCl or sodium bicarbonate orally. Oral rehydration therapy = mixture of NaCl, KCl and their citrates.
  • Sodium bicarbonate: usually oral, to regulate serum pH (e.g. renal tubular acidosis). In RTA the kidneys fail either to filter/secrete H⁺ (secretion in the distal tubule) or to recover HCO₃⁻ from the filtrate (passive re-absorption in the proximal tubule, active at the distal tubule). The active component is the bicarbonate anion; Na⁺ is responsible for solubility and compatibility.
  • Diet: most common source of NaCl is table salt; recommended limit 6 g/day for adults, significantly lower for children.
  • Hyponatremia (kidney dysfunction or sodium loss in the bowels): osmotic imbalance; signs: low blood pressure, dehydration, muscle cramps. Hypernatremia: cardiovascular disorders such as hypertension.
  • Acute toxicity: after ingestion of 500–1000 mg/kg NaCl; vomiting, GI ulceration, renal damage; high salt intake is believed to increase kidney-stone risk.

Potassium

  • 95% of K⁺ is intracellular, 5% mainly in blood plasma; balance maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump.
  • Hypokalemia causes: reduced intake from GI disturbance (diarrhea, vomiting) or increased excretion by diuresis (loop diuretics and thiazides increase Na⁺ secretion in nephrons to increase water excretion, but also K⁺ excretion). Potassium-sparing diuretics preserve K⁺.
  • Hyperkalaemia: plasma window 3.5–5.0 mmol; K⁺ salts can cause nausea, vomiting, and in extreme cases small bowel ulceration. Acute severe = plasma K⁺ > 6.5 mmol/l or ECG changes → can lead to cardiac arrest, needs immediate treatment. In kidney disease or end-stage renal failure, monitor K⁺ very carefully.

Processes / Stages

Aufbau filling order

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s → 5f → 6d → 7p.

Lithium blood level (plasma Li⁺)

  1. Therapeutic: narrow window; blood monitoring essential.
  2. > 1.5 mmol/l: toxic effects (finger tremors, renal impairment, convulsion).
  3. Extreme doses: nausea and diarrhea.
  4. > 2 mmol/l: emergency treatment (fatal).

Response to low blood pressure / low Na⁺

Kidneys sense the decrease → hormones released (renin, ADH, atrial natriuretic peptide) → control of blood pressure, osmotic balance, water retention.

Renal handling relevant to pH (RTA)

Filtration/secretion of H⁺ (secretion in distal tubule) and recovery of HCO₃⁻ (passive in proximal tubule, active at distal tubule) → failure = renal tubular acidosis.

Examples

  • Oxygen (Z 8): 8 protons, 8 electrons. Chlorine (Z 17): 17 protons, 17 electrons. Fluorine: mass number 19, atomic number 9.
  • Shorthand: Na (11) = 3s¹; Li (3) = 2s¹.
  • Aufbau examples: H 1s¹; He 1s²; Li 1s²2s¹; Be 1s²2s²; B 1s²2s²2p¹.
  • Alkali metal + water: 2Li(s) + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂(g)↑.
  • Medicinal alkali salts: NaCl, KCl (oral rehydration salts; KCl for potassium depletion), NaHCO₃, potassium bicarbonate, sodium citrate, potassium citrate, Li₂CO₃, lithium citrate [Li₃C₃H₅O(COO)₃].
  • Potassium citrate (UK, OTC): relief of discomfort in mild urinary-tract infections by increasing urinary pH.
  • Drugs in hyperkalaemia: calcium gluconate, soluble insulin, diuretics, polystyrene sulfonate resins.

Clinical / Practical Importance

Lithium

  • Advantages: prophylaxis and treatment of mania; prophylaxis in BD and recurrent depression; taken orally as Li₂CO₃ or lithium citrate, total dose up to 30 mmol/day.
  • Disadvantages: very narrow therapeutic index; memory problems, slowed mental ability, forgetfulness; weight gain; decreased thyroid levels; diabetes insipidus (polyuria due to damage of internal kidney structures).
  • Drug-drug interactions: NSAIDs reduce renal lithium clearance → toxicity. Diuretics (sodium depletion) worsen lithium toxicity and can be hazardous.

Sodium

  • NaCl solutions (mostly IV) for sodium depletion/dehydration; oral NaCl or NaHCO₃ for chronic mild to moderate loss; NaHCO₃ orally for serum pH regulation.
  • Sodium deficiency signs: low blood pressure, dehydration, muscle cramps.

Potassium

  • KCl: preferred K salt, preferably liquid; other salts if hyperchloremia risk. Potassium bicarbonate: oral, for chronic acidosis (impaired kidney function); must be carefully evaluated because even small changes in plasma K⁺ can have severe consequences.
  • Potassium citrate must not be given to men with pain in the kidney area (risk of kidney stones) or blood/pus in urine. Patients with raised blood pressure or diabetes should avoid it without consulting their GP.
  • K⁺ tablets/capsules (non-prescription): formulated for slow K⁺ release because very high K⁺ is toxic to tissue cells and injures gastric mucosa; usually < 100 mg K⁺.
